# Diuretics Made Simple — Thiazide, Loop, Potassium-Sparing + NCLEX Tips

A patient on furosemide has a potassium of 3.1. Another is on spironolactone with a potassium of 5.8. A third is on hydrochlorothiazide and their lithium level just spiked. Which one do you call the provider about first?

Diuretics show up on every nursing exam because they're prescribed constantly and their side effects can spiral fast. The key isn't memorizing every drug name — it's understanding what each class does to your electrolytes. Once you know the pattern, the questions answer themselves.

## The three classes at a glance

| Class | Site of Action | Electrolyte Effect | Key Drugs |
|-------|---------------|-------------------|-----------|
| Thiazide | Distal convoluted tubule | ↓ K, ↓ Na, ↓ Mg, ↑ Ca | HCTZ, chlorthalidone |
| Loop | Ascending loop of Henle | ↓ K, ↓ Na, ↓ Mg, ↓ Ca | Furosemide, bumetanide, torsemide |
| Potassium-sparing | Collecting duct | ↑ K (risk of hyperkalemia) | Spironolactone, eplerenone, amiloride, triamterene |

Here's the thing: thiazides and loops both waste potassium. Potassium-sparing drugs hold onto it. That's the entire electrolyte game in one sentence.

## Loop diuretics — the heavy hitters

Furosemide (Lasix) is the most tested drug in nursing school. It's powerful, fast-acting, and easy to mess up if you're not watching labs.

**How it works:** Blocks sodium-potassium-chloride cotransport in the thick ascending loop of Henle. This dumps massive amounts of sodium, potassium, chloride, and water into the urine.

**What gets wasted:** Potassium, sodium, magnesium, AND calcium. That calcium loss is unique to loops — thiazides actually hold onto calcium. This matters because loops can worsen osteoporosis with long-term use.

**NCLEX traps with furosemide:**

1. **The potassium trap:** Furosemide + digoxin is a dangerous combo. Low potassium makes digoxin toxic at normal doses. If you see both drugs on a med list, check that potassium level. Below 3.5? Hold both and call.

2. **The BP trap:** Furosemide IV can drop BP fast. Always check BP before giving it. Systolic under 90-100? Hold and notify. This drug can tank pressure in minutes.

3. **The hearing trap:** High-dose IV furosemide is ototoxic, especially when pushed too fast. The rule: give IV furosemide at 20 mg/min or slower. Ringing in the ears? Stop and report.

4. **The sulfa trap:** Furosemide is a sulfonamide. If your patient has a sulfa allergy, check the order. Cross-reactivity exists but is debated — some providers will still order it, others won't. Know your facility policy.

**Nursing priorities on loop diuretics:**
- Daily weights — same scale, same time, same clothes
- Strict I&O
- Monitor K, Na, Mg, Ca
- Check BP before administration
- Assess for dehydration: dry mucous membranes, poor skin turgor, orthostatic hypotension
- Give in the morning so the patient isn't up all night

## Thiazide diuretics — the first-line HTN agents

Hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ) and chlorthalidone are first-line for hypertension. They're milder than loops but still deplete potassium.

**How it works:** Blocks sodium-chloride cotransporter in the distal convoluted tubule.

**The calcium difference:** Unlike loops, thiazides REABSORB calcium. This makes them useful in patients with kidney stones (they reduce urinary calcium). But it also means they can cause hypercalcemia in susceptible patients.

**NCLEX traps with thiazides:**

1. **The lithium interaction:** Thiazides reduce lithium clearance. A patient stable on lithium can become toxic when you add HCTZ. Lithium levels must be monitored closely. This is classic NCLEX — patient on lithium starts HCTZ, lithium level spikes, now they have tremor, confusion, nausea. What happened? Thiazide reduced clearance.

2. **The gout trap:** Thiazides increase uric acid and can trigger gout flares. Gout patient? Thiazides are relatively contraindicated.

3. **The glucose trap:** Thiazides can raise blood glucose. Diabetic patients need closer monitoring.

**Mnemonic for thiazide side effects:** "HYPER" — **H**ypercalcemia, h**Y**perglycemia, h**Y**peruricemia, h**Y**perlipidemia. The first three are tested constantly.

## Potassium-sparing diuretics — the K protectors

Spironolactone is the star here. It blocks aldosterone, so you hold onto potassium instead of wasting it.

**How it works:** Aldosterone antagonist (spironolactone, eplerenone) or direct ENaC blocker (amiloride, triamterene).

**The danger:** Hyperkalemia. These drugs hold onto potassium. Combine them with an ACE inhibitor, ARB, or potassium supplements and potassium can climb dangerously high.

**NCLEX traps with spironolactone:**

1. **The combo trap:** Spironolactone + lisinopril + potassium supplement = hyperkalemia waiting to happen. The NCLEX loves this triad. Always check for duplicate potassium-raising therapies.

2. **The hormone trap:** Spironolactone blocks aldosterone AND androgen receptors. Men can get gynecomastia (breast tenderness, enlargement). Women can get menstrual irregularities. This is a famous side effect — if a male patient on spironolactone complains of breast tenderness, you know what's happening.

3. **The salt substitute trap:** Tell patients to avoid salt substitutes. They're made of potassium chloride. A patient on spironolactone using "low sodium" salt substitute can become dangerously hyperkalemic.

**Monitoring on potassium-sparing diuretics:**
- Potassium levels — before starting and regularly after
- Do NOT give potassium supplements unless specifically ordered and monitored
- Avoid salt substitutes
- Teach patients which foods are high in potassium (bananas, oranges, potatoes, tomatoes, avocados) — they don't need to eliminate them, but they should be aware

## The NCLEX pattern for diuretic questions

Every diuretic question follows the same logic:

1. **Identify the drug class** → Know which electrolytes are at risk
2. **Check the labs** → Is potassium high or low? What about sodium? Calcium?
3. **Look for dangerous combos** → Furosemide + digoxin? HCTZ + lithium? Spironolactone + ACE inhibitor?
4. **Prioritize** → Hyperkalemia (cardiac arrest risk) beats hypokalemia. Acute changes beat chronic ones.
