# Insulin Types NCLEX Guide — Onset, Peak, Duration Made Simple

Your patient got regular insulin at 0700. It's now 1000 and they're diaphoretic, confused, and have a glucose of 48. What happened?

Insulin timing questions are on every NCLEX because insulin errors are one of the most common causes of preventable harm in hospitals. Knowing the peak time of each insulin type isn't just exam trivia — it's how you keep patients alive.

## The four insulin types — by speed

| Type | Examples | Onset | Peak | Duration |
|------|----------|-------|------|----------|
| Rapid-acting | Lispro (Humalog), Aspart (Novolog), Glulisine (Apidra) | 15 min | 1-2 hrs | 3-5 hrs |
| Short-acting | Regular (Humulin R, Novolin R) | 30-60 min | 2-4 hrs | 5-8 hrs |
| Intermediate | NPH (Humulin N, Novolin N) | 2-4 hrs | 4-12 hrs | 12-18 hrs |
| Long-acting | Glargine (Lantus), Detemir (Levemir), Degludec (Tresiba) | 1-4 hrs | Peakless | 24-42 hrs |

## Rapid-acting insulin — mealtime coverage

Lispro, aspart, and glulisine are the "fast" insulins. They're given WITH meals or right after eating.

**The rule: Give rapid-acting insulin when the patient starts eating, not before.** If you give it 30 minutes before a meal and the patient doesn't eat, they'll crash. This is the most common insulin error in hospitals.

**NCLEX traps with rapid insulin:**

1. **The NPO trap:** Patient is NPO for a procedure. Their rapid-acting insulin is scheduled with breakfast. Do you give it? No. Hold it. The patient isn't eating. Giving insulin to an NPO patient is a direct path to hypoglycemia.

2. **The post-meal trap:** Lispro works so fast that it can be given after eating. If a patient ate half their meal, you can adjust the dose based on what they actually consumed. This is different from regular insulin, which needs to be given 30 minutes before.

**Mnemonic for rapid-acting:** "15 minutes feels like an hour when you're waiting" — onset is 15 minutes, peak at 1-2 hours.

## Short-acting (regular) insulin — the IV-compatible one

Regular insulin is the ONLY insulin that can be given IV. It's used for DKA treatment, sliding scale coverage, and in insulin drips.

**NCLEX traps with regular insulin:**

1. **The IV trap:** Regular insulin is the only one safe for IV administration. If a question asks about IV insulin for DKA, the answer is regular insulin. Not lispro, not glargine — regular.

2. **The timing trap:** Regular insulin is given 30 minutes before meals. If you give it at 0730 and breakfast arrives at 0800, that's fine. If breakfast is delayed, the patient could become hypoglycemic while waiting.

3. **The mixing trap:** When mixing regular insulin with NPH, draw up regular first (clear before cloudy). Air goes into NPH first, then regular. Draw up regular, then NPH. The mnemonic: RN — Regular before NPH.

**Mnemonic for regular insulin peak:** "2-4 hours." Think of it as the "afternoon crash" window — if regular insulin was given at lunch (1200), the peak hits between 1400 and 1600.

## NPH insulin — the intermediate workhorse

NPH is cloudy. You MUST roll it between your hands (don't shake — it creates bubbles) to resuspend it before drawing up.

**NCLEX traps with NPH:**

1. **The peak trap:** NPH peaks at 4-12 hours. That's a wide window, and it means the peak can sneak up on you. A patient who got NPH at 0800 could bottom out anywhere between noon and 2000. The NCLEX wants you to know that the peak is unpredictable — scheduled snacks are often ordered to cover it.

2. **The mix trap:** NPH is the only insulin that can be mixed with regular insulin in the same syringe. Rapid-acting and long-acting insulins are never mixed with anything.

3. **The bedtime snack trap:** NPH given at bedtime peaks in the early morning hours (0200-0400). This is when hypoglycemia is most likely. Bedtime snacks are ordered to prevent this. If a patient refuses their bedtime snack, check their glucose more frequently overnight.

## Long-acting insulin — the basal backbone

Glargine (Lantus), detemir (Levemir), and degludec (Tresiba) provide a steady background insulin level. They don't have a true peak — just a flat, sustained release.

**NCLEX traps with long-acting insulin:**

1. **The mixing trap:** Glargine is NEVER mixed with other insulins. It's a clear solution with an acidic pH. Mixing it with anything neutralizes the pH and ruins the slow-release mechanism. This is tested constantly.

2. **The timing trap:** Glargine is usually given at bedtime. Same time every night. Consistency is key. If a patient normally gets it at 2200 but it's now 0200 and they remember they missed it, call the provider — don't just give it late without orders.

3. **The DKA trap:** Long-acting insulin is NOT used for DKA. DKA needs IV regular insulin. Long-acting is too slow to correct metabolic emergencies.

4. **The brand confusion trap:** Lantus (glargine) and Levemir (detemir) sound different but do the same thing. Tresiba (degludec) lasts even longer — up to 42 hours. If a patient misses a dose of degludec, they have more flexibility in when to take the next dose because of the long duration. This is sometimes tested.

## The hypoglycemia protocol — every nurse's reflex

Insulin → hypoglycemia. It's the most common adverse effect, and the NCLEX will test your response:

1. **Check glucose** (fingerstick)
2. **If glucose < 70 and patient is conscious:** Give 15g fast-acting carbs — 4 oz juice, 3-4 glucose tablets, or 8 oz milk. Recheck in 15 minutes. Repeat if still low.
3. **If glucose < 70 and patient is unconscious or cannot swallow:** Do NOT put anything in their mouth. Give IV dextrose (D50) or IM glucagon. Position patient on their side (risk of aspiration).
4. **Once glucose normalizes:** Give a snack with protein and complex carbs to prevent rebound hypoglycemia.

**NCLEX trick question:** A patient with hypoglycemia is awake and can swallow. Do you give them orange juice with pulp, apple juice, or a candy bar? Apple juice — fastest absorption. Candy bars have fat that delays absorption. Orange juice with pulp has fiber that slows it.

## The insulin administration checklist

- **Right insulin** (check the vial — sound-alike names are a real problem)
- **Right dose** (insulin is a high-alert medication — double check)
- **Right time** (relative to meals or bedtime)
- **Right route** (SQ for most, IV only for regular)
- **Right site** (rotate sites — abdomen, thigh, arm, buttock. Absorption varies by site)
- **Right technique** (pinch skin, 45-90° angle depending on body habitus, do not massage)

## Quick reference — insulin peaks

| Insulin | When hypoglycemia is most likely |
|---------|----------------------------------|
| Lispro/Aspart | 1-2 hours after injection |
| Regular | 2-4 hours after injection |
| NPH | 4-12 hours after injection |
| Glargine/Detemir | No distinct peak (steady release) |

The NCLEX will describe a patient becoming confused, diaphoretic, and tachycardic at a specific time after their insulin dose. Match the time to the peak, identify the insulin type, and you know exactly what happened.
