# Electrolyte Imbalances Made Simple — Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+

Electrolyte questions show up on **every** NCLEX. You can bank on it. Understanding what goes wrong, what it looks like, and what the nurse does about it is one of the highest-yield areas in all of nursing pharmacology.

This guide covers the four electrolytes you'll see most: **potassium (K⁺)**, **sodium (Na⁺)**, **calcium (Ca²⁺)**, and **magnesium (Mg²⁺)**. For each one: normal values, signs of imbalance, ECG changes, causes, and nursing interventions.

---

## Potassium (K⁺) — Normal: 3.5–5.0 mEq/L

Potassium is the **heart rhythm electrolyte**. When it's off, the heart is the first thing you worry about.

### Hypokalemia (< 3.5 mEq/L)

**Mnemonic: "A SIC WALT"** — Alkalosis, Shallow respirations, Irritability, Confusion, Weakness, Arrhythmias, Lethargy, Thready pulse

| Sign | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Cardiac | Flat T waves, U waves, ST depression |
| Neuromuscular | Muscle weakness, cramping, hyporeflexia |
| GI | Decreased motility, ileus, constipation |
| Respiratory | Shallow breathing, respiratory failure (severe) |

**Causes:** Diuretics (furosemide), GI losses (vomiting, diarrhea, NG suction), inadequate intake, alkalosis

**Nursing interventions:**
- **NEVER push IV potassium** — it's lethal. Always dilute and infuse slowly
- Oral replacement preferred when possible (K-Dur, K-Lor)
- Monitor ECG continuously during IV replacement
- Check magnesium — you can't fix K⁺ if Mg²⁺ is low
- Encourage high-potassium foods: bananas, oranges, potatoes, spinach, avocados

### Hyperkalemia (> 5.0 mEq/L)

| Sign | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Cardiac | **Peaked T waves (early)** → widened QRS → sine wave → V-fib/cardiac arrest |
| Neuromuscular | Muscle twitching → weakness → flaccid paralysis |
| GI | Nausea, diarrhea, hyperactive bowel sounds |

**Causes:** Renal failure, potassium-sparing diuretics (spironolactone), ACE inhibitors, tissue damage (burns, crush injury), acidosis

**Nursing interventions:**
1. **Calcium gluconate** — first thing given. Stabilizes cardiac membrane (does NOT lower K⁺)
2. **Insulin + D50** — shifts K⁺ into cells
3. **Sodium bicarbonate** — especially if acidotic
4. **Kayexalate** — removes K⁺ through GI tract
5. **Dialysis** — definitive treatment if kidneys aren't working
- Stop all K⁺ supplements and K⁺-sparing diuretics
- Continuous cardiac monitoring

## Sodium (Na⁺) — Normal: 135–145 mEq/L

Sodium is the **brain electrolyte**. When it shifts, neurological symptoms dominate.

### Hyponatremia (< 135 mEq/L)

**Mnemonic: "SALT LOSS"** — Seizures/Stupor, Anorexia, Lethargy, Tendon reflexes decreased, Limp muscles, Orthostatic hypotension, Seizures, Stomach cramping

| Sign | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Neurological | Confusion, headache, lethargy → seizures, coma |
| Muscular | Muscle cramps, weakness |
| Cardiovascular | Hypotension, tachycardia |

**Causes:** SIADH, diuretics, excessive water intake, CHF, vomiting/diarrhea

**Nursing interventions:**
- **Correct SLOWLY** — rapid correction causes osmotic demyelination syndrome (central pontine myelinolysis). Maximum 10–12 mEq/L per 24 hours
- Fluid restriction if dilutional
- Hypertonic saline (3% NaCl) only in severe cases — ICU, slow infusion
- Monitor neuro status hourly
- Daily weights, strict I&O

### Hypernatremia (> 145 mEq/L)

| Sign | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Neurological | **SHRINK** — the brain cells shrink: Seizures, Headache, Restlessness, Increased reflexes, Nausea, K-coma |
| Mucous membranes | Dry, sticky mucous membranes |
| Cardiovascular | Tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension |

**Causes:** Dehydration, diabetes insipidus, excessive sodium intake, hypertonic tube feedings

**Nursing interventions:**
- Correct SLOWLY — cerebral edema risk if too fast
- Replace fluids: D5W or hypotonic solutions
- Monitor neurologic status and serum sodium frequently
- Oral water replacement if alert and able to swallow

## Calcium (Ca²⁺) — Normal: 8.5–10.5 mg/dL

Calcium keeps nerves firing and muscles contracting. When it's low, everything gets twitchy.

### Hypocalcemia (< 8.5 mg/dL)

**Mnemonic: "CATS go numb"** — Convulsions, Arrhythmias, Tetany, Spasms, Numbness/tingling

| Sign | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Neuromuscular | **Chvostek's sign** (tap facial nerve → facial twitch), **Trousseau's sign** (BP cuff → carpopedal spasm), paresthesia, tetany |
| Cardiac | Prolonged QT interval |
| Respiratory | Laryngospasm (emergency!) |

**Causes:** Hypoparathyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, renal failure, loop diuretics, massive blood transfusion (citrate binds calcium)

**Nursing interventions:**
- IV calcium gluconate or calcium chloride (calcium chloride is more potent — watch for extravasation)
- Oral calcium + vitamin D for maintenance
- Seizure precautions
- Monitor for laryngospasm — have emergency equipment ready

### Hypercalcemia (> 10.5 mg/dL)

**Mnemonic: "Bones, stones, groans, and psychiatric overtones"**

| Sign | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Bones | Bone pain, fractures |
| Stones | Kidney stones |
| Groans | Constipation, nausea, abdominal pain |
| Psychiatric | Confusion, lethargy, depression |
| Cardiac | Shortened QT interval |

**Causes:** Hyperparathyroidism, cancer (bone metastases), thiazide diuretics, excessive calcium/vitamin D

**Nursing interventions:**
- Aggressive IV fluids (normal saline) — promotes calcium excretion
- Loop diuretics (furosemide) — avoid thiazides (they retain calcium)
- Calcitonin, bisphosphonates in severe cases
- Monitor cardiac rhythm
- Ambulation and fluids to prevent stone formation

## Magnesium (Mg²⁺) — Normal: 1.5–2.5 mg/dL

Magnesium is the **gatekeeper** — you can't fix potassium or calcium until magnesium is normal.

### Hypomagnesemia (< 1.5 mg/dL)

| Sign | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Neuromuscular | **Looks exactly like hypocalcemia** — Chvostek's, Trousseau's, tetany, hyperreflexia |
| Cardiac | Torsades de pointes, prolonged QT, PVCs |
| GI | Nausea, vomiting, anorexia |

**Causes:** Alcoholism, malnutrition, diuretics, GI losses, pancreatitis

**Nursing interventions:**
- IV magnesium sulfate — infuse slowly (watch for respiratory depression)
- Oral magnesium supplements for maintenance
- **Fix magnesium BEFORE trying to fix potassium or calcium**
- Seizure precautions
- Monitor deep tendon reflexes

### Hypermagnesemia (> 2.5 mg/dL)

| Sign | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Neuromuscular | **Everything goes quiet** — hyporeflexia → absence of reflexes, muscle weakness, drowsiness |
| Cardiac | Bradycardia, hypotension, prolonged PR/QT |
| Respiratory | Respiratory depression in severe cases |

**Causes:** Renal failure, excessive magnesium intake (antacids, laxatives), treatment of preeclampsia

**Nursing interventions:**
- **Calcium gluconate** — antagonist, given IV
- Stop all magnesium-containing medications
- Dialysis if severe
- Monitor respiratory status and cardiac rhythm
- Check DTRs hourly

## NCLEX Practice Questions

**1.** A patient with a potassium level of 6.8 mEq/L is admitted to the telemetry unit. Which order should the nurse implement FIRST?

A. Administer Kayexalate 30g PO  
B. Start IV insulin with D50  
C. Give calcium gluconate IV  
D. Initiate hemodialysis consult

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Give calcium gluconate IV.** Calcium stabilizes the cardiac membrane and prevents life-threatening arrhythmias. It does NOT lower potassium — it buys time for the other interventions (insulin/D50, Kayexalate, dialysis) to work. On an NCLEX priority question, always stabilize the heart first.
</details>

**2.** The nurse is assessing a patient receiving IV magnesium sulfate. Which finding requires immediate intervention?

A. Deep tendon reflexes 2+  
B. Respiratory rate of 10 breaths/minute  
C. Blood pressure 110/70  
D. Urinary output 800 mL in 8 hours

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Respiratory rate of 10 breaths/minute.** Magnesium toxicity causes respiratory depression. The first sign of toxicity is loss of deep tendon reflexes — but once RR drops below 12, you have a true emergency. Stop the infusion and call the provider immediately.
</details>

**3.** A patient with SIADH is at highest risk for which electrolyte imbalance?

A. Hypernatremia  
B. Hypokalemia  
C. Hyponatremia  
D. Hypercalcemia

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Hyponatremia.** SIADH = Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone. The body holds onto too much water, diluting sodium. Treat with fluid restriction and slow sodium correction. Rapid correction causes osmotic demyelination.
</details>
