# Anticoagulants NCLEX Guide — Heparin, Warfarin, DOACs Made Simple

A patient on heparin has an aPTT of 120. Another on warfarin has an INR of 4.8. A third on rivaroxaban just fell and hit their head. What do you do first?

Anticoagulants are the most high-stakes drug class in nursing because the consequences of getting it wrong are catastrophic — bleeding or clotting, both potentially fatal. The NCLEX tests anticoagulants relentlessly because they require you to connect lab values, patient assessment, and nursing action in real time.

## The four categories at a glance

| Drug | Route | Lab | Reversal | Onset |
|------|-------|-----|----------|-------|
| Heparin (UFH) | IV/SQ | aPTT | Protamine sulfate | Immediate (IV) |
| Enoxaparin (LMWH) | SQ only | Not routinely monitored | Protamine (partial) | 3-5 hours |
| Warfarin | PO | PT/INR | Vitamin K | 3-5 days |
| DOACs (rivaroxaban, apixaban, etc.) | PO | Not routinely monitored | Andexanet (Xa), idarucizumab (dabigatran) | 2-4 hours |

The most important thing to understand: heparin works fast but wears off fast. Warfarin takes days to kick in but lasts for days. That's why patients starting warfarin are "bridged" with heparin — heparin protects them immediately while warfarin builds up.

## Heparin — the hospital workhorse

Unfractionated heparin (UFH) is the IV anticoagulant used in hospitals for acute clots, surgeries, and dialysis. Low molecular weight heparin (enoxaparin/Lovenox) is the SQ version used for DVT prophylaxis and stable patients.

**How it works:** Binds to antithrombin III, which then inactivates thrombin (factor IIa) and factor Xa. The end result: your clotting cascade shuts down.

**Lab monitoring:** aPTT — activated partial thromboplastin time. Normal aPTT is 25-35 seconds. Therapeutic on heparin is 1.5-2.5 times normal, so roughly 46-70 seconds depending on the lab.

**NCLEX traps with heparin:**

1. **The aPTT trap:** aPTT is for heparin, PT/INR is for warfarin. Mix these up and you're wrong. Remember: **A**PTT for hep**A**rin. Both start with the same letter pattern.

2. **The HIT trap:** Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia. After 5-10 days on heparin, platelets can suddenly plummet below 100,000 or drop 50% from baseline. This is an immune reaction, and it's dangerous because it causes CLOTTING (not bleeding). Platelets drop but the patient is at risk for new clots. Stop heparin immediately. Switch to a direct thrombin inhibitor like argatroban. The NCLEX tests this constantly.

3. **The SQ vs IV trap:** Enoxaparin (Lovenox) is SQ only. You can't give it IV. But regular heparin can be given either way. Know which form you're dealing with.

4. **The epidural trap:** Patients on enoxaparin should NOT have an epidural catheter removed until 12+ hours after the last dose. The risk is a spinal/epidural hematoma, which can cause permanent paralysis. This is a sentinel event.

5. **The reversal trap:** Heparin bleeding → protamine sulfate. That's the only answer. But protamine only partially reverses enoxaparin.

**Nursing priorities for heparin:**
- Check aPTT every 6 hours (or per protocol) when on IV drip
- Monitor platelets — watch for HIT
- Check all injection sites, IV sites, gums for bleeding
- Hemoccult/guaiac all stools
- No IM injections — they can cause hematomas
- Have protamine sulfate available on the unit

## Warfarin — the outpatient anticoagulant

Warfarin (Coumadin) is the old standby. It's cheap, effective, and annoying — constant lab draws, diet restrictions, drug interactions everywhere.

**How it works:** Blocks vitamin K-dependent clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X). Without vitamin K, the liver can't make these factors.

**Lab monitoring:** PT/INR — prothrombin time expressed as International Normalized Ratio. Normal INR is about 1.0. Therapeutic for most indications is 2.0-3.0. Mechanical heart valves may need 2.5-3.5.

**NCLEX traps with warfarin:**

1. **The vitamin K trap:** Warfarin OD → vitamin K. But vitamin K takes 6-24 hours to work. For life-threatening bleeding, you need fresh frozen plasma (FFP) or prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC). Vitamin K alone isn't enough for an active brain bleed. This distinction is tested.

2. **The diet trap:** Vitamin K antagonizes warfarin. Foods high in vitamin K: kale, spinach, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, green tea. The key teaching point: consistency, not avoidance. Eat the same amount of greens each week. Don't binge on kale smoothies or suddenly cut all greens.

3. **The drug interaction trap:** Warfarin interacts with everything. Antibiotics (kill gut bacteria that make vitamin K), amiodarone, NSAIDs, PPIs, statins, herbal supplements (St. John's Wort, ginkgo, garlic, ginseng). The rule: any new medication means more frequent INR checks.

4. **The pregnancy trap:** Warfarin is Pregnancy Category X — never in pregnancy. It crosses the placenta and causes fetal warfarin syndrome. Pregnant women who need anticoagulation get heparin or enoxaparin instead.

5. **The bridge trap:** Starting warfarin takes 3-5 days to reach therapeutic INR. During that time, the patient is at risk for clots (warfarin initially causes a brief hypercoagulable state by depleting protein C first). That's why they're bridged with heparin until INR is therapeutic for 2 consecutive days.

**Patient education for warfarin:**
- Same time every day (usually evening)
- Never double dose
- Keep diet consistent, not restricted
- Avoid alcohol (affects warfarin metabolism and increases bleeding risk)
- Use soft toothbrush, electric razor
- Report any bleeding: nosebleeds, bleeding gums, bruising, dark/tarry stools, blood in urine
- Medical alert bracelet — always
- Tell every provider (dentist, surgeon, pharmacist) you're on warfarin

## DOACs — the new generation

Direct Oral Anticoagulants include rivaroxaban (Xarelto), apixaban (Eliquis), edoxaban (Savaysa), and dabigatran (Pradaxa). They're easier than warfarin — no routine labs, fewer diet restrictions, fewer drug interactions.

**How they work:** Direct factor Xa inhibitors (rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban) or direct thrombin inhibitor (dabigatran).

**Monitoring:** Routine labs not needed. But they DO affect PT/aPTT — you just don't titrate to a target. Renal function matters because all DOACs are renally cleared to some degree.

**NCLEX traps with DOACs:**

1. **The renal trap:** DOACs are renally cleared. Poor kidney function = drug accumulates = bleeding risk. Always check creatinine clearance before administration.

2. **The reversal trap:** Until recently, DOACs had no reversal agents. Now: andexanet alfa (Andexxa) reverses factor Xa inhibitors. Idarucizumab (Praxbind) reverses dabigatran. But these are expensive and not available everywhere. The NCLEX may test that you know reversal exists but also that you should check availability.

3. **The switch trap:** Switching from warfarin to DOAC or vice versa requires careful timing. Don't overlap in a way that causes supratherapeutic anticoagulation. Follow the specific protocol for each drug.

4. **The compliance trap:** DOACs have short half-lives. Missing even ONE dose leaves the patient unprotected. This is different from warfarin, which lingers for days. Patient education on strict compliance is essential.

## Comparing the labs

| Anticoagulant | Lab to Monitor | Therapeutic Range |
|--------------|----------------|-------------------|
| Heparin (UFH) | aPTT | 1.5-2.5 × normal |
| Enoxaparin (LMWH) | Anti-Xa (if needed) | Per protocol |
| Warfarin | PT/INR | 2.0-3.0 (most indications) |
| DOACs | None routine | N/A |

## The NCLEX anticoagulant decision tree

When you see an anticoagulant question, follow this order:

1. **Which drug?** → This tells you the lab and reversal agent
2. **What are the labs?** → Are they therapeutic, subtherapeutic, or supratherapeutic?
3. **What's the patient doing?** → Bleeding? Clotting? Just maintaining?
4. **What's the priority?** → Active bleeding > risk of clotting > routine monitoring

**Red flag values:**
- aPTT > 100 on heparin → hold infusion, call provider, consider protamine
- INR > 4.0 on warfarin → hold dose, call provider, give vitamin K if bleeding
- Platelets drop 50% on heparin → suspect HIT, stop heparin, draw HIT antibody
