# ACE Inhibitors Made Simple — Cough, Potassium & the NCLEX Traps That Get Everyone

Your patient is on lisinopril. Their potassium comes back at 5.8. They're also 12 weeks pregnant and asking if they should keep taking it "since their blood pressure is finally normal."

If you can spot all three problems in that scenario, you're ready. If any of those made you nervous, this is for you.

ACE inhibitors are the most-prescribed antihypertensives in the United States. They show up on every NCLEX. But most study guides bury the useful stuff under 10 pages of RAAS pathway diagrams. That's not what you need. You need to know: why the cough happens, what kills potassium, when to hold the drug, and the three NCLEX traps that catch people every single time.

## What are ACE inhibitors?

ACE inhibitors are blood pressure medications that end in "-pril." Lisinopril. Enalapril. Ramipril. Captopril. Benazepril. Quinapril.

They're usually the first drug a provider reaches for when someone has hypertension, heart failure, or kidney protection needs — especially in diabetes.

The name stands for **A**ngiotensin-**C**onverting **E**nzyme inhibitors. They block the enzyme that makes angiotensin II, which is your body's most powerful blood-vessel-squeezer. No angiotensin II means blood vessels stay relaxed. Blood pressure drops. The heart has less work to do.

Simple enough. But here's where it gets interesting.

## How they actually work (in plain English)

Your kidneys have a pressure sensor. When blood pressure drops too low, the kidneys release a hormone called renin. Renin kicks off a chain reaction:

```
Liver makes angiotensinogen (inactive)
       ↓
Renin converts it → angiotensin I (still inactive)
       ↓
ACE enzyme converts it → angiotensin II (active troublemaker)
```

Angiotensin II does three things:
1. Squeezes blood vessels tight (vasoconstriction) — raises BP
2. Tells the adrenal glands to release aldosterone, which makes your kidneys hold onto sodium and water — raises BP more
3. Tells your kidneys to dump potassium

ACE inhibitors stop step 3. They block that converting enzyme so angiotensin I never becomes angiotensin II.

No angiotensin II = vessels relax, kidneys release water and sodium, and potassium stays in the blood.

That last part is critical. Potassium stays in. If you're also giving the patient a potassium supplement, or a potassium-sparing diuretic like spironolactone, potassium can climb fast. More on that in a minute.

**The cough.** ACE also normally breaks down something called bradykinin — an inflammatory chemical that makes vessels dilate. When you inhibit ACE, bradykinin piles up. That buildup irritates the lungs and throat, causing a dry, hacking cough that won't go away. About 10-20% of patients get it. It's not dangerous, but it's annoying enough that providers usually switch the patient to an ARB (losartan, valsartan) instead. [^1]

**The analogy:** Think of your blood vessels as a hallway. Angiotensin II is the guy pushing everyone against the walls. ACE inhibitors fire that guy. Now people can spread out and move freely. Bradykinin is the co-worker who got promoted and immediately started coughing on everyone. ARBs are the new hire who doesn't have the cough problem.

## Clinical uses

- **Hypertension** — first-line for most patients, especially those under 60
- **Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction** — makes it easier for the heart to pump by lowering the resistance it pushes against (afterload)
- **Post-MI** — started within 24 hours of a heart attack to protect the heart muscle and prevent remodeling
- **Diabetic nephropathy** — slows kidney damage in patients with diabetes by reducing pressure inside the glomeruli
- **Chronic kidney disease with proteinuria** — reduces protein loss regardless of diabetes status

## Nursing considerations

**Nursing Alert:** Check potassium and creatinine before the first dose and within 1-2 weeks of starting or increasing. An ACE inhibitor can double someone's potassium if they also have kidney issues or are on other potassium-raising drugs.

**Nursing Alert:** If the patient's face, lips, tongue, or throat swells — this is angioedema. Stop the drug. Secure the airway. Call the provider immediately. This is not a "monitor and see" situation. Angioedema from ACE inhibitors is more common in Black patients but can happen to anyone. [^2]

What you monitor:
- **Blood pressure** — especially after the first dose. The first-dose phenomenon can drop BP low enough to make them dizzy or syncopal
- **Potassium** — normal is 3.5-5.0 mEq/L. ACE inhibitors push it up. Don't give potassium supplements or salt substitutes (which are potassium chloride) without checking
- **Renal function** — BUN and creatinine. A small bump in creatinine (up to 30%) after starting is expected and usually ok. A big jump means trouble
- **Cough** — dry, persistent, non-productive. Ask: "Have you developed a tickle in your throat that won't clear?" If yes and it bothers them, the provider may switch to an ARB

**Nursing Alert:** Pregnancy is an absolute contraindication. ACE inhibitors have a black box warning for fetal toxicity. They can cause fetal kidney failure, skull defects, and death — especially in the second and third trimesters. If your patient is pregnant or planning to become pregnant, this drug stops immediately. [^3]

Drug interactions to know:
- **NSAIDs** (ibuprofen, naproxen) — blunt the BP-lowering effect and increase kidney risk
- **Potassium-sparing diuretics** (spironolactone, eplerenone) — hyperkalemia risk skyrockets
- **Lithium** — ACE inhibitors reduce lithium clearance, pushing lithium levels toward toxicity
- **Diuretics** — additive hypotension, especially after the first dose

Patient teaching:
- Don't stop suddenly — blood pressure can rebound higher than before
- Avoid salt substitutes (they're usually potassium chloride)
- Change positions slowly — orthostatic hypotension is real
- Report any lip, face, or throat swelling immediately
- The cough is annoying but not dangerous. Tell your provider — don't just stop taking it.

## NCLEX traps: the 3 questions that get everyone

### Trap 1: The hyperkalemia distraction

*The patient is on lisinopril and spironolactone. Potassium comes back at 5.9. The patient's ECG shows peaked T waves. What's the priority?*

The NCLEX will give you a list of reasonable options. Call the provider. Hold both drugs. Give kayexalate. Check another lab.

The answer is **hold both drugs** and call the provider. The combination of an ACE inhibitor and a potassium-sparing diuretic is a recipe for hyperkalemia. Peaked T waves on the ECG mean it's already affecting the heart. The priority is stopping the cause.

What the NCLEX is testing: Do you actually connect the drug mechanism (ACE inhibitors raise potassium) to the clinical scenario? Or do you just pick the first option that sounds nurse-like?

### Trap 2: The pregnancy question

*A 26-year-old woman on lisinopril for hypertension discovers she's 8 weeks pregnant. She asks if she should continue the medication since her blood pressure has been well-controlled. What do you say?*

If you said anything other than "stop the drug and switch to something pregnancy-safe," you fell for it.

The NCLEX loves this one because it tests whether you know the black box warning versus just knowing the drug "isn't recommended" during pregnancy. ACE inhibitors are not "use with caution" drugs in pregnancy. They're "do not use" drugs. Fetal risk of renal failure and death.

The follow-up the NCLEX sometimes throws: *What antihypertensive is safe in pregnancy?* The answer is methyldopa, labetalol, or nifedipine — depending on what other options they give you.

### Trap 3: The cough vs. heart failure symptom

*A patient on enalapril for heart failure reports a new dry cough that keeps them up at night. The nurse notes fine crackles in the lung bases and an O2 sat of 91%. What should the nurse do first?*

The NCLEX will give you options like: notify the provider about switching to an ARB, or assess further for signs of worsening heart failure.

The right answer: assess further. Yes, ACE inhibitors cause cough. But this patient has heart failure. A cough plus crackles plus dropping O2 sat could mean fluid is backing up into their lungs. Don't just blame the drug. Rule out the life-threatening thing first.

This is the NCLEX testing your ability to prioritize — is this a side effect or a complication of the disease?

## Memory hook: A.C.E.

Next time you see a "-pril" drug, think **A.C.E.**:

- **A**ngioedema — face swelling = emergency. Get help now.
- **C**ough — dry, annoying, caused by bradykinin. Not dangerous but tell the provider.
- **E**levated potassium — check the labs. Watch for salt substitutes. Never pair with spironolactone without a good reason.

Three letters. Three things that can go wrong. You now know more about ACE inhibitors than 90% of the students in your cohort.

## Quick reference table

| What | Details |
|------|---------|
| **Drug names** | "-pril" drugs: lisinopril, enalapril, ramipril, captopril, benazepril, quinapril |
| **Class** | ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitors |
| **Mechanism** | Blocks conversion of angiotensin I → angiotensin II. Decreases vasoconstriction, decreases aldosterone (less sodium/water retention), increases bradykinin |
| **Onset** | Varies by drug. Captopril: 15-30 min. Lisinopril: 1-2 hours. Enalapril: 1 hour |
| **Peak** | Captopril: 1-1.5 hours. Lisinopril: 6-8 hours. Enalapril: 4-6 hours |
| **Duration** | Captopril: 6-12 hours. Lisinopril: 24 hours. Enalapril: 12-24 hours |
| **Route** | PO (most), captopril can be given sublingual in emergencies |
| **Key labs** | Potassium (3.5-5.0), BUN (5-20), creatinine (0.6-1.2 mg/dL), GFR |
| **Antidote** | No direct antidote. For overdose: IV fluids, vasopressors if needed. For angioedema: airway management, epinephrine, antihistamines, steroids |
| **Pregnancy** | Category D — contraindicated. Black box warning for fetal toxicity |
| **Major interactions** | Potassium supplements, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, lithium, ARBs (don't double up) |
