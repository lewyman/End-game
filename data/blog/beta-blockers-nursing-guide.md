# Beta Blockers Made Simple — Nursing Tips for Metoprolol, Atenolol & Propranolol

Beta blockers show up in every nursing class, every NCLEX practice test, and every clinical — but they're also one of the easiest drug classes to mix up. Here's the no-nonsense breakdown.

## The Core Mechanism

Beta blockers block beta-adrenergic receptors. There are two flavors:

- **Beta-1** → Heart (cardio-selective)
- **Beta-2** → Lungs + vascular smooth muscle (non-selective)

If a drug only blocks Beta-1, you get the heart effects without messing with the lungs. If it blocks both, you get cardiovascular effects *plus* bronchoconstriction risk.

## The Three You Must Know for NCLEX

### 1. Metoprolol (Lopressor) — Cardio-Selective (Beta-1)

- **Used for:** hypertension, angina, heart failure, post-MI
- **Key assessment:** Check HR and BP before administering. Hold if HR < 60 or systolic BP < 90.
- **NCLEX pearl:** Metoprolol can mask hypoglycemia symptoms in diabetics. Tachycardia is a warning sign that goes away — teach patients to check blood glucose, not just rely on symptoms.
- **Adverse effects:** bradycardia, hypotension, fatigue, dizziness, depression
- **Patient teaching:** Do not stop abruptly — can cause rebound hypertension and tachycardia. Taper off.

### 2. Atenolol (Tenormin) — Cardio-Selective (Beta-1)

- **Used for:** hypertension, angina, post-MI
- **Key difference from metoprolol:** Atenolol is less lipid-soluble → less CNS penetration → fewer CNS side effects (less fatigue, less vivid dreams)
- **NCLEX pearl:** Same hold parameters as metoprolol. Same diabetes caution.
- **Administration:** Can give with or without food. Renally excreted — watch kidney function.

### 3. Propranolol (Inderal) — Non-Selective (Beta-1 + Beta-2)

- **Used for:** hypertension, angina, migraine prophylaxis, essential tremor, performance anxiety, thyrotoxicosis
- **The big difference:** Blocks Beta-2 → causes bronchoconstriction. Contraindicated in asthma and COPD.
- **NCLEX pearl:** Propranolol crosses the blood-brain barrier easily → more CNS effects: fatigue, depression, vivid dreams, insomnia
- **Also:** Can cause peripheral vasoconstriction (cold hands/feet) from unopposed alpha activity

## NCLEX Mnemonic: "M-A-P the Beta Blockers"

| Letter | Drug | Selectivity | Watch For |
|---|---|---|---|
| **M** | Metoprolol | Beta-1 (heart) | Masks hypoglycemia |
| **A** | Atenolol | Beta-1 (heart) | Kidney function |
| **P** | Propranolol | Both | No asthma/COPD |

## Nursing Interventions Before Giving

1. **Check vitals** — HR and BP every time
2. **Count apical pulse** for 1 full minute if < 60
3. **Hold and notify** if HR < 60 or systolic < 90
4. **Monitor** for signs of heart failure exacerbation (edema, crackles, weight gain)
5. **Teach** diabetic patients to monitor blood glucose, not rely on tachycardia as a warning sign

## Key Drug Interactions

- **Calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem)** → additive bradycardia and heart block risk
- **Insulin and oral hypoglycemics** → beta blockers mask hypoglycemia symptoms
- **NSAIDs** → reduce antihypertensive effect

## Patient Teaching Points

- Take pulse daily before each dose — report < 60 to provider
- Change positions slowly (orthostatic hypotension risk)
- Do NOT stop abruptly — taper under provider supervision
- Report: severe dizziness, fainting, shortness of breath, weight gain > 2 lbs/day
- Avoid OTC cold medications without checking — many contain vasoconstrictors

## Quick NCLEX Question

*A patient on metoprolol 50 mg daily has a heart rate of 56 bpm and blood pressure of 108/72. The patient feels fine. What should the nurse do?*

**Answer:** Hold the dose and notify the provider. HR < 60 is the hold parameter regardless of how the patient feels.
