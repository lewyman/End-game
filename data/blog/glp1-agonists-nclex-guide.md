# GLP-1 Drugs on the NCLEX? What Every Nursing Student Must Know About Ozempic, Mounjaro & the New Generation

## What Are GLP-1 Receptor Agonists?

GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) is a natural hormone your gut releases after eating. It does three things:

1. **Stimulates insulin secretion** — but only when blood glucose is high (glucose-dependent). This is the magic: they don't cause hypoglycemia on their own.
2. **Suppresses glucagon** — stops your liver from dumping extra glucose into the bloodstream.
3. **Slows gastric emptying** — food stays in the stomach longer, so you feel full.

GLP-1 receptor agonists are synthetic versions of this hormone. They bind to GLP-1 receptors and produce the same effects — amplified and prolonged.

**The key NCLEX concept:** These drugs are glucose-dependent insulin secretagogues. Unlike sulfonylureas (which force insulin release regardless of blood sugar), GLP-1 drugs only trigger insulin when glucose is elevated. That's why hypoglycemia risk is low with monotherapy — but rises if combined with insulin or sulfonylureas.

## Mechanism Deep Dive: What the NCLEX Expects You to Know

The NCLEX won't ask you to recite molecular pathways, but it will test whether you understand the *clinical consequences* of how these drugs work.

### The "Incretin Effect" Explained

When you eat, your gut releases incretin hormones (GLP-1 and GIP). These signal your pancreas to release insulin. In type 2 diabetes, the incretin effect is blunted. GLP-1 agonists restore and amplify this signal.

**Think of it this way:** GLP-1 drugs are like a bouncer at a club. When glucose is high, the bouncer opens the door for insulin. When glucose is normal, the door stays shut. No glucose, no insulin release. This is fundamentally different from sulfonylureas, which kick the door down regardless.

### Tirzepatide: Dual Agonist

Tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound) activates both GLP-1 *and* GIP receptors. GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide) enhances insulin secretion and improves insulin sensitivity. The dual action produces greater A1C reduction and weight loss than GLP-1 alone. In head-to-head trials, tirzepatide outperformed semaglutide for both endpoints.

### Retatrutide: The Triple Agonist (Coming Soon)

Presented at the American Diabetes Association 86th Scientific Sessions on June 6, 2026, retatrutide is the first drug to activate GLP-1, GIP, *and* glucagon receptors. The glucagon component increases energy expenditure — something existing obesity drugs don't do.

**TRIUMPH-1 trial results (80 weeks, 12 mg dose):**
- Average weight loss: 70.3 lbs (28.3% of body weight)
- 45.3% of participants achieved ≥30% weight loss (bariatric surgery territory)
- In the 104-week extension, participants with BMI ≥35 lost up to 85 lbs (30.3%)

**TRANSCEND-T2D-1 results (40 weeks):**
- A1C reduction up to 2.0%
- Weight loss up to 36.6 lbs (16.8%)
- Up to 46% of participants achieved a normal A1C

**NCLEX implication:** If retatrutide is approved (filings expected late 2026), it will appear in NCLEX questions. Know that it's a *triple* agonist and adds glucagon-mediated energy expenditure to the GLP-1/GIP mechanism.

## NCLEX Traps & High-Yield Points

### Trap #1: "GLP-1 drugs cause hypoglycemia"

**Wrong.** As monotherapy, GLP-1 agonists rarely cause hypoglycemia because they're glucose-dependent. The NCLEX will try to trick you by listing hypoglycemia as a major side effect — it's not, *unless* the patient is also on insulin or a sulfonylurea.

> **NCLEX tip:** If you see a patient on a GLP-1 drug alone with low blood sugar, look for another cause — they skipped a meal, exercised excessively, or took too much of another diabetes drug. The GLP-1 didn't cause it.

### Trap #2: "The post-op patient can take their GLP-1 as scheduled"

**Dangerous.** GLP-1 drugs delay gastric emptying. This means food — and gastric contents — sit in the stomach longer. For a patient undergoing anesthesia, this creates a serious aspiration risk even if they've been NPO for the standard 6-8 hours.

**Guideline:** Hold GLP-1 agonists before elective surgery. The American Society of Anesthesiologists recommends holding daily GLP-1s on the day of surgery and weekly GLP-1s for at least one week before. Residual gastric volume can be significant even after prolonged fasting.

> **NCLEX tip:** A patient says "I took my Ozempic yesterday and my surgery is tomorrow morning." This is a red flag — notify the anesthesia team. Aspiration pneumonia is preventable.

### Trap #3: "Nausea is just an annoying side effect"

Nausea is the most common side effect of GLP-1 drugs — affecting 20-45% of patients depending on the drug and dose. But the NCLEX tests whether you recognize that nausea can lead to:

1. **Volume depletion** — vomiting + reduced oral intake = dehydration, AKI risk
2. **Malnutrition** — chronic reduced intake in elderly or frail patients
3. **Medication non-adherence** — patients stop taking the drug

**Nursing response:** Slow dose titration, small frequent meals, avoid high-fat foods, take with food if PO (Rybelsus), and monitor for dehydration. Document weight trends.

### Trap #4: "It's fine for anyone who wants to lose weight"

GLP-1 drugs are contraindicated in:

- **Personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC)** — black box warning
- **Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2)** — black box warning
- **Pancreatitis (active or history)** — can worsen
- **Pregnancy** — weight loss offers no benefit and may cause fetal harm
- **Severe GI disease** — gastroparesis is a relative contraindication

> **NCLEX tip:** The MTC/MEN 2 black box warning is a classic "which patient should NOT receive this drug?" question. If you see thyroid cancer or MEN 2 in the patient history, that's your answer.

### Trap #5: "You can switch between brands without any adjustments"

Different GLP-1 drugs have different dosing schedules and titration requirements. Switching from liraglutide (daily) to semaglutide (weekly) requires a washout period and starting the new drug at the initial dose — not the maintenance dose.

## Nursing Considerations

### Before Administration

1. **Check renal function** — GLP-1 drugs are renally excreted (semaglutide, liraglutide) or have renal dose adjustments (exenatide). Acute kidney injury has been reported, usually secondary to dehydration from GI side effects.
2. **Assess GI history** — gastroparesis, pancreatitis, gallbladder disease.
3. **Review concurrent diabetes medications** — if on insulin or sulfonylurea, doses may need reduction when starting a GLP-1.
4. **Pregnancy test** if indicated — discontinue at least 2 months before planned pregnancy (semaglutide washout is long due to half-life).

### During Treatment

1. **Monitor for pancreatitis** — severe abdominal pain radiating to the back, nausea/vomiting, elevated lipase/amylase. Discontinue if suspected.
2. **Monitor for gallbladder disease** — GLP-1 drugs increase risk of cholelithiasis and cholecystitis, likely due to rapid weight loss.
3. **Monitor weight and A1C** — these are your effectiveness markers. Weight loss typically plateaus at 6-12 months.
4. **Assess for depression/suicidal ideation** — rare but reported. Screen at follow-up visits.
5. **Injection site reactions** — rotate sites (abdomen, thigh, upper arm). Lipohypertrophy can occur with repeated use of the same site.

### Patient Education

- **"Start low, go slow"** — dose titration reduces GI side effects. Don't skip ahead.
- **Take on the same day each week** (weekly formulations) — if you miss a dose and it's been <5 days, take it. If >5 days, skip and wait for the next scheduled dose.
- **Storage:** Unopened pens in the refrigerator. Opened pens can be kept at room temperature for 28-56 days (varies by brand — check the label).
- **Recognizing pancreatitis:** Teach them to report severe, persistent abdominal pain immediately.
- **Thyroid tumors:** Explain the black box warning — report any neck mass, hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, or shortness of breath.
- **Dietary guidance:** Small, frequent meals. Stop eating when full. Avoid greasy/fatty foods. Stay hydrated.
- **Surgery/procedures:** Tell every healthcare provider you're on a GLP-1, especially before anesthesia.

## The Retatrutide Chapter: What's Coming

The NCLEX adapts to clinical reality. With retatrutide's Phase 3 data breaking records — and an expected FDA submission in late 2026 — this drug will enter NCLEX question banks within 1-2 testing cycles after approval.

**Key facts for future NCLEX:**
- **First triple agonist** (GIP + GLP-1 + glucagon)
- **Unique mechanism:** Adds increased energy expenditure (glucagon receptor) to appetite suppression and glycemic control
- **Weight loss magnitude:** 28-30%, comparable to bariatric surgery
- **Side effect profile:** Similar GI effects to existing GLP-1s (nausea 28-42%, diarrhea, vomiting), but at higher rates with higher doses
- **Not yet approved** — currently investigational

## Putting It All Together: NCLEX-Style Clinical Scenarios

**Scenario 1:** A patient with type 2 diabetes on metformin and semaglutide (Ozempic) 1 mg weekly reports persistent nausea and has lost 8 lbs in 3 weeks. Their A1C has improved from 8.2% to 7.1%. What should the nurse do first?

*Answer: Assess for dehydration and food intake patterns. Nausea is expected during dose titration, but rapid weight loss plus nausea should trigger assessment for volume depletion and inadequate nutrition. The drug is working (A1C improved), so don't immediately discontinue — but don't ignore the side effects either. Consider a slower titration schedule or antiemetic therapy.*

**Scenario 2:** A patient on tirzepatide (Mounjaro) 10 mg weekly is scheduled for a colonoscopy with sedation in 3 days. They took their last dose 2 days ago. What's the priority?

*Answer: Notify the gastroenterologist/anesthesia team. Tirzepatide delays gastric emptying and should be held at least 1 week before procedures requiring sedation. The patient's last dose was only 5 days pre-procedure — high risk for residual gastric contents and aspiration.*

**Scenario 3:** The nurse is teaching a patient starting liraglutide (Victoza) for type 2 diabetes. Which statement by the patient indicates understanding?

*Options:*  
*A) "I should check my blood sugar more often because this drug can cause low blood sugar."*  
*B) "I'll inject this once a week in my thigh or abdomen."*  
*C) "If I miss a dose, I should take two doses the next day."*  
*D) "This drug will help me feel full sooner when I eat."*

*Correct answer: D. GLP-1 drugs slow gastric emptying and promote satiety. A is wrong — hypoglycemia risk is low with monotherapy. B is wrong — Victoza is daily, not weekly. C is wrong — never double-dose GLP-1 drugs.*

## You've Got This

Pharmacology can feel like drinking from a fire hose, but GLP-1 receptor agonists actually make sense when you understand the underlying logic. They mimic a natural hormone your body already produces — they just amplify it. The side effects are predictable (GI, GI, and more GI). The contraindications are memorable (thyroid cancer, pancreatitis, pregnancy). And the NCLEX patterns repeat predictably.

**What to drill before exam day:**
- Which GLP-1 is weekly? (Most of them — dulaglutide, semaglutide, tirzepatide, exenatide Bydureon)
- Which is daily? (Liraglutide, exenatide Byetta)
- Which is oral? (Semaglutide as Rybelsus — and it's daily, not weekly)
- Which patient should never get one? (MTC/MEN 2 history)
- What's the pre-op concern? (Delayed gastric emptying → aspiration risk)

When you're sitting in the exam room and a GLP-1 question pops up, take a breath. Walk through the mechanism: slow stomach → full feeling → less eating → weight loss + better glucose. Walk through the safety checklist: thyroid history? Pancreatitis? Pregnant? Pre-op? If none of those, your patient is probably in good shape.

You've already done the hard part — you're here, studying, doing the work. The NCLEX is not testing whether you've memorized every drug; it's testing whether you can think like a nurse. And thinking like a nurse means understanding that a drug that slows gastric emptying is a drug you hold before surgery. It means knowing that "glucose-dependent" insulin release means something fundamentally different from "forced" insulin release. That's clinical judgment, and you're building it right now.
